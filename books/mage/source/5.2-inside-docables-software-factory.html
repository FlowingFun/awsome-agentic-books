    Chapter 5 — DocAble Among Other Software Factories  ::  §5.2

    §5.2 Inside DocAble's Software Factory

    This section illustrates

    ✓ The Modeling Principle · ✓ The Alignment Principle · ✓ Governance Conversion · ✓ Residual Human Judgment

    DocAble was built by one engineer (me) directing a fleet of coding agents. The product was a document-accessibility remediation service; the factory was the production system through which I directed changes to that service. I was engineering both. The product had to improve accessibility while preserving the document properties its users depended on. The factory had to organize implementation and preserve consequential engineering decisions as agents changed the system.

    The record examined here spans 31 complete weeks, from March 12 through October 14, 2026. At the final snapshot, DocAble contained 615,395 lines of production code and 2,333,471 lines of supporting source, including tests, checks, tools, infrastructure, models, and indexed documentation. The history identified 143 model modules. These measurements describe the resulting system and its engineering environment; they do not measure how much useful work each line or model represents.

    New work came from my goals for the system, user feedback, production failures, accessibility standards, public guidance from advocacy groups 11. National Federation of the Blind, Creating Nonvisually Accessible Documents, Access Technology Team, Jernigan Institute, Baltimore, MD, 2014, https://nfb.org/images/nfb/products_technology/creating_accessible_documents.docx., and the demands of institutional use. I decided which pressures warranted engineering work and made consequential decisions about requirements, architecture, validation, and deployment. Coding agents wrote every line of the implementation. I inspected almost none of the code they produced.

    That division of work required process design and tolerance. Work had to be selected, planned, dispatched, integrated, and evaluated without making me the bottleneck for every implementation decision. At the same time, agents needed freedom to choose implementations without being free to violate consequential requirements. The factory therefore developed models, procedures, tools, and controls that carried selected decisions into later work. A failure could require two repairs: changing the product and changing how future changes to it were governed.

    The case follows the factory’s development from initial requirements through production operation. Its chronology matters because the machinery did not arrive fully formed. Implementation, institutional use, and observed failures exposed engineering decisions that needed to persist; the factory changed to carry them.

    5.2.1 The Problem

    In 2024, the U.S. Department of Justice required state and local governments, including public universities and schools, to make covered web content and mobile applications conform to WCAG 2.1 Level AA 2.2. U.S. Department of Justice, Nondiscrimination on the Basis of Disability; Accessibility of Web Information and Services of State and Local Government Entities, 89 Fed. Reg. 31,320, 2024. The original compliance date for larger public entities was April 24, 2026. Four days before that deadline, the Department extended it by one year 3.3. U.S. Department of Justice, “Extension of Compliance Dates for Nondiscrimination on the Basis of Disability; Accessibility of Web Information and Services of State and Local Government Entities,” Federal Register 91 (2026): 20902–12, https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/04/20/2026-07663/extension-of-compliance-dates-for-nondiscrimination-on-the-basis-of-disability-accessibility-of-web.

    For a university, compliance creates a recurring production problem: courses distribute documents whose meaning must remain available to readers using assistive technology. Purdue trained undergraduates to remediate course materials, providing both a practical reference workflow and the corpus used in the evaluation below. A checker can identify some defects, but supplying a useful description of a diagram or restoring the structure of a document requires interpreting its content. Chapter 1 introduced that difficulty; here it becomes a specification for a service.

    The requirements were ordinary production-software requirements. What was unusual was the implementation capacity available to one engineer.

    §1.3 grouped these obligations by kind to establish the nature of the problem. Table 5.2-1 states the eight requirements: they explain why demonstrating semantic reasoning was only the beginning.

    Table 5.2-1. What DocAble had to accomplish. The eight requirements, R1–R8 — the specification the factory had to satisfy, which §1.3 introduced by kind.
    RequirementDocAble must…
    R1 · Multi-format remediationRemediate educational documents in the formats people actually use, including Office (PowerPoint, Word, Excel) and PDF.
    R2 · Semantic remediationPerform semantic repairs that require understanding document content, not only syntactic, deterministic transformations.
    R3 · Preserve the documentRetain content, appearance, and behavior except where remediation requires a change.
    R4 · Standards-grounded accessibilityEvaluate and repair documents against their governing obligations (WCAG 2.1 AA), not proxy checker scores.
    R5 · EvidenceRecord what accessibility requirement motivated each change and what evidence supports the resulting document state.
    R6 · ReviewabilityMake outputs auditably traceable: consequential automated changes are inspectable and, where appropriate, reversible.
    R7 · Production operationProcess real documents at practical sizes, costs, and latency.
    R8 · Institutional deploymentOperate as a deployed service that scales to thousands of faculty at one institution, and preferably nationwide.

    These requirements explain why the resulting system grew large. Semantic reasoning was only one requirement of eight. A frontier foundation model could supply a capability that had previously been missing. That capability alone did not establish preservation, reviewable changes, conformance, or dependable service operation; each remained an engineering obligation. Those obligations generated the engineering system around that capability.

    5.2.2 Building the Factory

    The Feasibility Probe

    I tested the uncertain capability first. I gave a multimodal foundation model pictures of mathematical equations, technical slides, and handwritten notes. On those examples it produced useful transcriptions and alternative text. The results supplied initial evidence that generative AI could perform some of the semantic reasoning needed for remediation; they did not establish that it could reliably remediate arbitrary documents.

    I decided not to make the foundation model the whole remediation system. To use its semantic capability in a service, I would also have to establish document preservation, reviewable changes, conformance, dependable operation, and practical cost.

    The Two Rules of Agentic Software Engineering

    I began with two rules.

    Reading the code is the last resort. If building DocAble required me to inspect the implementation produced by coding agents, then implementation throughput would remain bounded by my capacity to read code. I wanted to build an engineering environment in which I could direct work, understand the system, evaluate evidence, and decide whether changes were acceptable without routinely reconstructing those answers from implementation.

    This did not mean that I refused to read source code. It meant that needing to read it pointed to a missing supervisory surface: a model, test, lint, instrument, architectural boundary, or other representation of the property I actually needed to reason about. Reading the code was the escape hatch, not the operating model.

    The resulting system should be good, fast, and cheap. This was partly a requirement for DocAble and partly a test of agentic software engineering itself. I wanted to know whether agents could help build a production system that was not merely fast to develop, but good enough to trust, fast enough to matter, and cheap enough to deploy at scale. I therefore made all three requirements for the factory.

    A fast factory producing poor software would fail the test. So would a high-quality system whose development or operation remained prohibitively expensive. Agentic implementation was valuable only if the engineering system around it could achieve all three.

    The Build, in Stages

    I began with PowerPoint, then generalized the system to Word and Excel and added PDF support as colleagues brought other documents (R1). Each new format tested assumptions that my own files had not exposed. Putting DocAble online introduced authentication, deployment, institutional use, and unfamiliar workloads (R8). Users then found outputs that passed existing checkers while losing semantic equivalence, prompting validation grounded more directly in accessibility standards (R4). When the Department of Justice extended the deadline, I shifted more attention toward production hardening (R7).

    Figure 5.2-1 records the sequence. The stages mark changes in the engineering problem, not merely larger quantities of implementation. Supporting another format could reveal a weakness in the shared architecture; serving users could reveal a missing obligation that no development fixture had exercised.

    One operational surprise came from Google Cloud. I had asked Claude to deploy DocAble, and it did. What I had not specified was the full deployment lifecycle, including how obsolete deployments should be retired. Eventually I noticed that the service was costing roughly $40 per day when I expected something closer to $5. I asked Claude to investigate. It reported approximately 2,000 obsolete deployments that had accumulated without being cleaned up. The cloud bill was about $1,000.

    The repair was to complete the lifecycle model by adding a cleanup step for obsolete deployments. Claude had done what I asked—and had neglected what I had omitted.

    The Build, Measured Weekly

    The repository reconstruction covers 31 complete seven-day weeks from March 12 through October 14, 2026 4.4. James C. Davis, DocAble: Automated Document-Accessibility Remediation, Good Fast and Cheap, SEIP manuscript, October 28, 2026, 2026. Figure 5.2-2 brings four dimensions together: landed work commits, accumulated models and checks, recovered agent dispatches, and documented Epic completions. They describe the activity and structure of the factory; none alone measures productivity.

    An Epic represented a substantial engineering intention with a rationale, design, relevant invariants, phases, and acceptance criteria. Nontrivial designs received separate review using another foundation model, and a Definition-of-Done review assessed completion. One Epic could require several dispatches and many commits. Across the complete window, the reconstruction recovers 36,578 work commits, 14,392 dispatches, and 428 affirmative completion claims.

    The final snapshot identifies 143 model modules, 45 consumers of at least two models, and 232 static and 153 dynamic verifier files. The inventory records an increasing investment in representations and checks; the examples below examine their engineering roles and consumers.

    Week 11 marks a deliberate change in my engineering practice. Notes from May 22 and 24 describe replacing repeated agent audits with deterministic checks, supported by typing, lint-authoring, and repair campaigns. The commit subjects and emerging formal Epic records agree with that account. They support an interpretation of a hardening campaign; the first surviving Epic or dispatch record does not establish when I first used agents, planning, or checks.

    The series require some care. Work commits are reconstructed from direct and expanded squash landings, with duplicates, empty commits, and integration bookkeeping excluded. Completion records are affirmative claims rather than independent outcome audits, and dispatch retention is uneven. Earlier model existence is traced by path so a later declaration convention does not create a false growth spike. Registered verifier files establish an available checking apparatus, not that every check ran.

    The Support Ratio

    The source composition gives a different view of the investment (Figure 5.2-3). Production includes remediation, web processing, the frontend, and shared services. The supporting apparatus includes tests, lints, tools, agent infrastructure, infrastructure-as-code, models, and indexed documentation. Historical snapshots and generated copies are excluded.

    Call apparatus divided by production the support ratio. At weeks 11, 21, and 31 it was approximately 2.6, 3.6, and 3.8, respectively; it reached about 4.5 at week 17. The last snapshot contains 2,333,471 apparatus lines supporting 615,395 production lines. These are measurements at ten sampled revisions, with interpolation between them. A larger ratio is not a target. Tests, models, and operating tools carry different obligations, and supporting source earns its upkeep only while later work benefits from it.

    Together, the measurements show sustained implementation accompanied by an expanding engineering environment. They do not isolate the cost of a requirement or the causal contribution of a control. They also leave a question for engineering judgment: would this project have been feasible under the same constraints without generative AI or the machinery built around it?

    Field note — The counterfactual

    Within the actual constraints of this project—one primary engineer, an academic budget, and the development interval reported here—DocAble was not feasible for me without generative AI. I would not have attempted a production system at this scope without commodity implementation capacity.

    Sustaining that delegation posed a second problem. Once the change volume exceeded what I could inspect conventionally, continuing required explicit representations, independent evidence, constraints, and gates. My judgment is that GenAI made the project feasible and the machinery that became MAGE made delegation at this rate feasible. This is an account of the project I could undertake, not a controlled estimate of either contribution.

    5.2.3 Inside the Factory

    As DocAble grew, source code alone stopped being an adequate engineering interface. Repository search could locate a symbol without explaining which services participated in a workflow, who owned a piece of state, or which resource bound a proposed change had to preserve. The growth in supporting source recorded in Figure 5.2-3 reflects my response to such problems. The following examples show what that investment made possible: representations that answered engineering questions directly, relationships from which the factory could derive checks and configuration, and controls that made selected obligations consequential for later changes.

    What the Factory Knows

    The 31-week reconstruction identifies 143 model modules at the final snapshot. These representations change with the system and answer different engineering questions. Registries describe ownership and available operations; budgets make resource bounds explicit; state machines expose legal transitions; decision tables preserve policy distinctions. Some models describe the product, while others describe the agent fleet and its engineering environment. The factory represents both the software it produces and the process that produces it.

    Each representation gives the engineer a purpose-built view of a consequential property. The model carries selected engineering knowledge that the supervisor can inspect directly, without reconstructing it from implementation. This applies the purposeful reduction developed in Chapters 2–4 to factory supervision. Table 5.2-2 shows six representative models, chosen for the variety of question, form, and consumer.

    The Joins: What the Factory Can Derive and Enforce

    In §2.6, DocAble used shared identities to answer engineering questions, such as how a particular deployment performed. In the factory, these joins also determine which properties need checking, which mechanisms check them, and what evidence a change must supply before it can proceed. The architecture resembles proof checking in one limited respect: a producer is separated from the smaller mechanism that decides whether its evidence is sufficient for admission 5.5. Talia Ringer et al., “QED at Large: A Survey of Engineering of Formally Verified Software,” Foundations and Trends in Programming Languages 5, nos. 2–3 (2019): 102–281, https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.06458. Three joins show the difference in increasing order of reach.

    Property facts → verification tier → required checker → blocking consequence. Each invariant in the cross-service state-machine model carries declared property facts: how many concurrent lanes touch it, whether it is a safety or a liveness property, what state it ranges over. From those facts the model derives a verification tier — an invariant crossing multiple concurrent lanes demands exhaustive state-space exploration; a single-lane invariant demands a property test; a temporal obligation demands a model checker run. The tier is not advice: a check walks the model and fails when a mandated checker does not exist on disk. The supervisor need not choose a checker class afresh for every invariant. The encoded rule derives that requirement from the declared property facts, and the environment rejects a missing checker.

    Service relationships × deployment wiring × runtime identity → an access-grant plan. The typed inter-service edge model declares which service calls which, over which plane, under which identity. Deployment machinery consumes those edges and derives the invoker grants and token audiences each service needs — and a coherence gate asserts at deploy time that the granted permissions match the modeled relationships. Least-privilege wiring is not a checklist a human re-audits; it is a projection of the service graph.

    Mutation → stamp → derived account. Every mutating operation in the remediation core writes a provenance stamp — the verb, the pass, the visibility of the change — and the customer-facing changelog is derived from those stamps rather than authored beside them. A wiring validator identifies mutating paths that fail to stamp their work. The derived changelog provides structured evidence of the recorded mutations; the wiring check guards the obligation to record them.

    Modeling changed what the factory could know. Alignment changed what that knowledge could cause.

    The Computation Graph: a Representation That Took Over Execution

    The remediation graph makes a further connection: its representation determines execution. Each pass declares typed input and output relationships; the graph derives dependencies from those declarations, and execution machinery uses the dependencies to determine when work is ready. A blocking parity check holds the declarations and projected graph together. Composition therefore need not be reconstructed from behavior scattered across pass bodies.

    §4.2 develops the architectural history, including the large presentation that exceeded page-chunked execution. Here the consequence is the mature capability: the same stable computation identities support execution and analytical views of critical paths, mutation boundedness, cost, and coverage. An analytical view can answer a question execution itself does not need to answer.

    Explicit models expanded the questions the environment could answer. A lifecycle model made illegal transitions explicit. An ownership model reduced a concurrency protocol to the states and interleavings needed to ask whether two workers could hold the same claim; a small purpose-built checker could then exhaustively explore that finite state space. Eventual termination — a property over executions, not states — was written as small temporal specifications and checked mechanically; with recovery disabled, the checker produced exactly the stranded execution the property was meant to exclude. Checking the model created a second obligation: why believe a property established over the model holds of the implementation? Conformance tests exercised production operations against the executable model, and when one such test drifted red for days after production changed, the durable response was correspondence machinery — model-to-implementation checks selected automatically when modeled surfaces change. The factory then added machinery to keep the map true.

    Substituting the Reasoning Engine

    DocAble separates the reasoning that proposes a repair from the machinery that changes the document. The separation preserves much of the engineering investment when the reasoning strategy changes: a new strategy can reuse the document models, controlled operations, and validation machinery rather than reconstructing them. It also lets us ask whether more general reasoning earns its additional cost.

    Substitution occurs at two levels. For individual tasks, ModelSelector assigns a foundation model using task-specific configuration and capability requirements. The remediation graph stays the same while the model supplying a judgment changes. A per-task benchmark compares candidate models on quality, cost, and latency. At a coarser level, a general-purpose agent can choose and sequence repairs instead of following the production graph’s orchestration.

    Selecting a foundation model was itself an engineering decision: frontier foundation models were reserved for judgments where their additional capability earned their additional cost — the cheapest-adequate-judge rule of §4.4 applied to the fabricator rather than to the validator.

    The PDF changeset interface gives these strategies a common way to request supported mutations. A proposed operation names its target and intended change; the applier resolves it against the current document, checks its preconditions, and records the outcome. The structured pipeline and an experimental agent can therefore reuse typed document operations, independent validation, and provenance while deciding differently what to repair. Human-directed editing uses the same operation vocabulary. Figure 5.2-4 shows these architectural alternatives; the experiments below concern automated strategies.

    This interface was still incomplete in November 2026. Dozens of PDF remediation passes retained direct-mutation paths, including repairs whose effects required additional operation types or more substantial restructuring. The common interface made some reasoning interchangeable; it did not make the entire pipeline replaceable. Extending the operation vocabulary was itself engineering work, especially where an edit changed several related structures or depended on targets created by an earlier edit.

    Experiments clarified the role of representation. Frontier agents inferred PDF structure through several tool interactions, while a foundation model given a prepared scaffold inferred structure in one pass on a separate prose fixture. The scaffolded pass achieved high agreement with its reference structure at much lower inference cost. Because the documents and interaction patterns differed, this was not a matched cost comparison at equivalent quality. It identified an architectural choice: which useful structure should the system supply, and which judgments should the foundation model make 4?

    I had expected that a more capable agent might sometimes lower total cost by inspecting the document more broadly and avoiding repeated specialized calls. An experiment in September 2026 compared the pipeline with an agent using DocAble’s document tools on one handwritten PDF. It pointed the other way on cost. The pipeline’s metered inference cost was about $0.0419; the estimated agent execution cost was $2.29.** The agent figure was reconstructed from its transcript, including cached-input charges, rather than read from an API billing meter. A modeled minimal-context loop cost $1.24; charging the whole experimental session gave $6.92. These are alternative accounting scopes, not separate measured strategies. Repeated processing of the agent’s accumulated context accounted for much of the estimate. Both approaches had produced outputs with no failures on the evaluated veraPDF checks. The agent recovered more of the reference document structure, but had seen the reference sequence; this cost experiment could not establish an unbiased quality comparison.

    A November evaluation tested a narrower question: could a coached repair loop improve an agent’s result? The loop inspected remaining findings and could request another repair pass. On two PDFs, it closed no additional findings beyond the single-pass agent. The harder document completed a second pass but retained the same 15 findings; its cost rose from $1.35 to $3.57. The other document had no remaining findings after one pass, so the loop stopped. The result did not justify promoting the loop to production. Both configurations already shared recently added document operations; a second round of reasoning supplied no measured benefit on this pair.

    The unresolved question is where additional reasoning earns its cost. Difficult semantic cases may justify a frontier agent, and an agent can help investigate failures or test what the specialized pipeline misses. Those uses require evidence about the quality gained as well as the inference bill. DocAble’s architecture lets us investigate them while retaining the document representations, supported operations, and validation machinery already built. That retained work is engineering capital: a new reasoning strategy can inherit it without reproducing the engineering decisions it embodies.

    5.2.4 The Factory in Operation

    From a Priority to Completed Work

    How does one engineer keep several agents working without continually prompting each one? The workflow described here is the factory’s November 3, 2026 configuration, following the measurements ending October 14. I record priorities and deployment prerequisites in a strategy document. An orchestrating agent selects ready work and coordinates its planning, implementation, and evaluation.

    A planning agent develops an epic: a persistent work order recording the change’s purpose, scope, phases, dependencies, and completion criteria. Its design document identifies consequential properties and the tests or analyses required for them. A separate agent reviews the plan; unresolved engineering decisions return to me. The work order preserves decisions across dispatches while leaving implementation choices within the stated requirements to the agents.

    The phase dependencies determine which agents can work concurrently, as Figure 5.2-5 shows. The orchestrator dispatches ready phases into isolated Git worktrees. Each agent first inspects its assigned component and records a refined plan, including target files, in its first commit. Before changing the implementation, it supplies those files to Query Repo, which retrieves the associated models, invariants, tests, and static checks. A pre-commit check requires a record that this discovery occurred. Known obligations can therefore inform the agent’s implementation choices from the outset.

    Completed changes pass inexpensive checks before integration. Broader validation runs periodically against the combined code, and the deployment process reruns the full validation suite. Two changes may each work in isolation and still fail when combined, so separate worktrees do not remove the need to check the integrated result. §4.4 explains the bottleneck that motivated this arrangement and the cost of delaying some checks.

    Finally, an independent agent audits the epic against its completion criteria. It checks that the expected changes landed and that the required evidence supports completion. Missing work or failed checks lead to repair and another review. Problems the agents cannot resolve, including defects in the factory itself, return to me. The work order closes after the audit accepts the result.

    What Escalated Failures Became

    Some of DocAble’s engineering models were designed from known requirements. Others arose when failures exposed missing distinctions or controls. Three episodes illustrate the latter process, involving document mutation, instructions to foundation models, and integration of concurrent development work.

    Preserving document structure required a controlled mutation boundary. An early PDF-slicing operation used PyMuPDF to copy pages into smaller documents. The resulting pages rendered correctly, but the operation discarded the PDF’s accessibility tag tree, including /StructTreeRoot and the page-to-structure relationships. A library call that preserved visible content had silently destroyed information essential to accessibility.

    The immediate repair replaced the slicing operation with one that preserved the tags. I then established a canonical-library policy and concentrated PDF mutations in typed operations under AdaTool.PdfModel. For example, structure insertion passes through PdfStructInserter rather than allowing arbitrary calls to the canonical PDF library’s tag-manipulation APIs. A blocking direct-access lint rejects those calls outside designated modules. The architectural model identifies where PDF structure may be changed; the lint checks that subsequent implementations preserve the boundary.

    Semantic instructions required their own engineering model. Agents developing DocAble’s image-description prompts introduced numerical requirements, including a demand for at least three specific facts and a minimum description length. Some source images did not contain enough legible information to satisfy those requirements. In several evaluated cases, the foundation model invented plausible values or padded descriptions to meet the prescribed quantity. The problem was not the particular threshold: an unconditional demand for information absent from the source could produce the same failure.

    I responded with a typed prompt-governance model. It classifies prompts by purpose, such as describing, transcribing, or extracting information, and classifies their instructions by the demands they impose. A decision table determines which combinations are permitted. For example, a minimum fact count is prohibited in a description prompt, while an extraction requirement may be permitted when conditioned on information actually present in the source. The model distinguishes requirements appropriate to a task from requirements that could make faithful completion impossible.

    A deterministic lint checks registered prompt definitions against this model and blocks illegal combinations. An agent introducing another unconditional fact minimum into a description prompt encounters a failed check rather than relying on a reviewer to recognize the mistake. The lint governs the instructions, not the descriptions produced from them; it cannot establish that an individual output contains no fabricated content. It prevents a recurring class of inappropriate requirements from entering the remediation system 4.

    Integration automation required a model of safe conflict resolution. DocAble’s agents worked concurrently in separate Git worktrees, often changing overlapping files. The integration machinery ordinarily rebased completed work onto the current repository. When a rebase encountered a conflict, an automatic retry used Git’s -X theirs option, favoring one side of each conflicting hunk. In June 2026, concurrent modifications to the integration machinery itself produced an incompatible combination of edits. The resulting Python code failed to load, disabling scheduled integration.

    The failure exposed a decision that the automation had been making without an adequate policy: which conflicts may be resolved automatically? I distinguished three cases. A clean rebase can proceed. Certain narrowly defined additive conflicts can use an established resolution procedure. Other conflicts require escalation because the integration machinery cannot establish that combining the changes preserves their intended effects. This decision model separates the presence of a conflict from permission to resolve it automatically. A conflict is evidence that further work may be needed, not authorization to discard one side.

    I removed the -X theirs retry and changed the integration procedure to follow this policy. Clean integrations and permitted additive resolutions could proceed automatically; unresolved conflicts produced a conflict-need-help disposition. That disposition made the remaining work suitable for delegation. Rather than having Git select a resolution without understanding the changes, the disposition provided a defined task that I could assign to an agent: examine the conflicting contributions, reconcile their intended effects, and produce a candidate for validation. The agent could perform the repair, but the integration procedure would not accept an arbitrary conflict resolution merely because it had been generated. The model thus governed both what the automation could decide on its own and how unresolved work could be handed back to an agent.

    Across these episodes, I repeatedly observed that coding agents could diagnose defects and implement repairs but seldom proposed the larger changes to models, architecture, or controls that would prevent similar failures. Claude suggested replacing the offending library call, not establishing a controlled mutation boundary. Claude suggested revising the prompt, not modeling which instructions were appropriate to different semantic tasks. Claude suggested repairing the integration script, not reconsidering which conflicts the factory should resolve automatically. I generally had to identify and direct these larger changes, although agents could implement the resulting machinery. Once the decisions were represented explicitly, agents could also undertake work that the new structure made delegable, such as reconciling conflicts identified by the integration policy. Bui and Evangelopoulos distinguish this capacity to execute assigned work from the proactivity needed to recognize worthwhile work independently 6.6. Nghi D. Q. Bui and Georgios Evangelopoulos, Agentic Coding Needs Proactivity, Not Just Autonomy, Google Research, 2026, https://research.google/pubs/agentic-coding-needs-proactivity-not-just-autonomy/. My experience illustrates that distinction, although it does not establish a general limitation of coding agents.

    5.2.5 The Supervisor's Job

    As DocAble’s engineering environment developed, my role changed with it. Figure 5.2-6 summarizes that progression in three stages: co-coder, tech lead, and architect. The staircase represents two related developments: the models and controls available to govern agent work accumulated, allowing me to supervise implementation at increasing levels of abstraction.

    Initially, I read, judged, and merged agents’ changes. As implementation outpaced my attention, I delegated evaluation to other agents, then replaced recurring decidable findings with deterministic checks. Specialized reviewers, named ownership zones, and boundary rules allowed me to direct work without inspecting every change. Eventually, architectural, behavioral, and ownership models provided system-level views for planning and evaluation. I could make decisions about the system without routinely reconstructing its implementation.

    For example, a cross-format migration touching 58 files and rewriting 5,000 lines took about 5 hours of agent time over a dinner break. My decision was that those files should change; the agents performed the migration. I rarely needed to inspect the implementation, although model drift still required correspondence checks, and failures in the engineering environment could return me to direct inspection.

    My attention shifted toward deciding what should change, which properties required representation or enforcement, and what anomalous evidence meant. The environment took over more of the routine work of directing and evaluating implementation, but decisions about system structure, acceptable risk, and unresolved failures remained mine.
