    Chapter 1 — The New Engineering Problem  ::  §1.3

    §1.3 MAGE by Example: DocAble

    The engineering methodology described in this book emerged from six months of developing and studying a production document-accessibility system called DocAble. I directed the development, but coding agents wrote every line of its implementation. My role was to exercise engineering judgment, direct the agents, and develop the models and controls governing their work. Chapter 5 examines the system and its development in detail. For now, a few facts about DocAble are in order.

    1.3.1 The Accessibility Problem

    In 2024, the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ) adopted a rule under Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA). This rule required covered state and local government entities, including public universities, to make their digital content accessible, whether delivered through the web or mobile applications. The rule sets the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG 2.1 Level AA) as the technical standard. The DOJ later extended the compliance deadline for larger public entities by one year, from April 2026 to April 2027 1.1. U.S. Department of Justice, “Extension of Compliance Dates for Nondiscrimination on the Basis of Disability; Accessibility of Web Information and Services of State and Local Government Entities,” Federal Register 91 (2026): 20902–12, https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/04/20/2026-07663/extension-of-compliance-dates-for-nondiscrimination-on-the-basis-of-disability-accessibility-of-web. The educational purpose is straightforward: students using assistive technologies, such as screen readers, should be able to obtain and use the information their classmates can access. The rule describes access in terms of “substantially equivalent timeliness, privacy, independence, and ease of use” 2.2. U.S. Department of Justice, Nondiscrimination on the Basis of Disability; Accessibility of Web Information and Services of State and Local Government Entities, 89 Fed. Reg. 31,320, 2024.

    A university’s digital content extends well beyond its public website. Faculty distribute lecture slides, readings, assignments, laboratory instructions, spreadsheets, and other materials through personal websites and learning-management systems. These documents come in many formats, including Microsoft Office (PowerPoint, Word, Excel), their LibreOffice equivalents, HTML, PDF, and LaTeX. Accessibility requires more than making their text machine-readable. A screen reader needs a meaningful reading order, figures need descriptions that convey their educational purpose, and tables and headings need structure that supports navigation 3.3. World Wide Web Consortium, Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.1, 2018, https://www.w3.org/TR/2018/REC-WCAG21-20180605/. One way to eliminate the problem would be to stop distributing documents to students altogether, but the students might take this amiss.** Of course, the U.S. Department of Justice might have taken an even dimmer view of inaccessible course documents. UC Berkeley removed thousands of publicly available lecture recordings in 2017 following a DOJ accessibility investigation.

    The scale makes manual remediation difficult. A single course may distribute dozens of slide decks and readings, amounting to hundreds or thousands of pages and slides. Multiply that workload by the thousands of courses offered by many universities, with materials revised each semester, and accessibility becomes a substantial continuing obligation. DocAble would need to improve accessibility without damaging the original documents, interpret their content accurately, explain its changes, and operate economically at institutional scale. Table 1.3-1 groups these requirements.

    Table 1.3-1. What DocAble had to accomplish, grouped into four kinds of obligation. Chapter 5 states the complete requirements this grouping summarizes.
    Kind of obligationDocAble must…
    Genuine accessibilityRepair problems that require understanding document content, judged against the governing standard (WCAG 2.1 AA) rather than proxy checker scores.
    PreservationRetain the document's content, appearance, and behavior except where remediation requires a change.
    AccountabilityRecord what motivated each change and what evidence supports the result, keeping consequential changes inspectable and, where appropriate, reversible.
    Practical operation at scaleHandle the formats people actually use, at practical sizes, costs, and latency, as a deployed service for thousands of faculty.

    1.3.2 The Challenge of Automated Accessibility

    Purdue had licensed tools that could identify accessibility problems in course materials, but they did not fix them. I personally maintain two courses containing hundreds of pages and slides, and had no desire to spend a semester remediating them manually. I could delegate some of that work to other people, but delegation would not make me any less responsible for the result. As a lazy software engineer, however, I was perfectly willing to spend six months attempting to automate the work to my own standard.

    In early 2026, coding agents had also become capable enough to make a different experiment possible. I wanted to discover what software engineering would look like if I delegated implementation to agents. Accessibility remediation provided a real problem on which to find out. From the user’s perspective, the intended service was simple: upload an inaccessible document; receive a remediated document and a changelog. DocAble would support Word, PowerPoint, Excel, their LibreOffice equivalents, PDF, and text-based formats including LaTeX and Typst. Where possible, it would describe figures, repair reading order, and restore structural accessibility while preserving the original content and presentation. Figure 1.3-1 shows the intended result.

    Deciding what to change requires understanding what the content contributes to the lesson. A blue square might decorate a restaurant menu but be the subject of a lecture on modern art. The same visual object calls for different treatment because its purpose differs. Foreign-language materials pose another problem. Language tagging can help a screen reader pronounce a French passage correctly, but supplying a translation that students are supposed to produce themselves would undermine the assignment. A scanned page of handwritten mathematics presents yet another challenge: making it accessible may require recovering notation in a form assistive technology can interpret, while preserving its mathematical meaning.

    These examples illustrate why accessibility remediation requires interpreting content in context. Generative AI can help with that interpretation, but building a reliable remediation system requires more. It must handle document formats correctly, preserve the author’s content and presentation, and provide evidence of what changed and what remains uncertain.
