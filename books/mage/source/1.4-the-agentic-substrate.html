    Chapter 1 — The New Engineering Problem  ::  §1.4

    §1.4 Agentic Machinery and Its Properties

    Engineering methods must fit the machinery they govern. Figure 1.4-1 distinguishes three components of an agentic engineering system: the foundation model that supplies general capabilities, the harness that turns those capabilities into actions, and the engineered environment in which agents work. Understanding their respective roles helps explain where engineers can exercise control.

    1.4.1 Foundation Models: The Source of Commodity Intelligence

    The coding agents available in 2026 are built primarily around large language models (LLMs), which serve as their foundation models. These models acquire general capabilities through training on large collections of text, software, and other data.** Engineering predates machine learning in its use of models as purposeful representations of systems. Machine learning later adopted the same word for a learned approximation of a function—also reasonably a model, but a different kind. This book gives the engineering usage precedence: unqualified model means a purposeful representation of an engineered system or some property of it. I therefore use foundation model for the trained machine-learning system and agent for the acting system built around one through a harness, tools, and working state. Where the distinction matters, I will not ask the reader to infer which “model” I mean. Publicly available source code, including open-source repositories, has been an important source of training material for coding models 1.1. Mark Chen et al., Evaluating Large Language Models Trained on Code, arXiv preprint arXiv:2107.03374, 2021, https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03374. Training mixtures can also include licensed material, and their full composition is not always disclosed 2.2. OpenAI, GPT-4 Technical Report, arXiv preprint arXiv:2303.08774, 2023, https://cdn.openai.com/papers/gpt-4.pdf.

    The goal of training is to develop a model that can make useful predictions and generalize beyond its training examples. Training typically begins with a prediction objective: given a sequence of tokens, predict the next one. Tokens are units of representation, often words, parts of words, or punctuation. Training adjusts numerical parameters, or weights, to improve these predictions. Additional training can improve how a foundation model follows instructions and responds to human requests 3.3. Long Ouyang et al., “Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback,” Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 35 (2022): 27730–44, https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/hash/b1efde53be364a73914f58805a001731-Abstract-Conference.html. At inference time, the trained model processes the context supplied to it and generates a response. An agentic harness turns some of those responses into actions, such as searching a repository, modifying files, running tests, or invoking development tools. Tool results can then be returned as context for further reasoning 4.4. Shunyu Yao et al., “ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting in Language Models,” International Conference on Learning Representations, 2023.

    A foundation model trained on public software may know programming languages, libraries, common architectures, and established engineering practices. It ordinarily will not know the current state of the particular system an engineer asks it to modify. That system may be proprietary, may have changed since training, or may contain local design decisions that general programming knowledge cannot supply. Even a seemingly local change may depend on an architectural boundary established years earlier, a configuration convention shared across services, or an operational constraint that appears nowhere near the code being edited. Agents must recover relevant knowledge from the codebase, documentation, configuration, and other available evidence. General programming knowledge must be connected to the particular system and the obligations its changes must preserve.

    Engineers can influence a language-based agent in two broad ways. They can modify the foundation model through training or fine-tuning, changing its weights. Alternatively, they can change what the agent receives and the environment in which it operates: instructions, system knowledge, tools, permissions, and feedback. The agentic harness provides some of the machinery for exercising this control, including context management, tool access, and hooks through which engineers can observe or constrain actions. Training a frontier foundation model requires resources far beyond those of an ordinary software engineering project. Fine-tuning is more accessible, particularly through techniques that update only a fraction of the model’s parameters 55. Edward J. Hu et al., “LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models,” International Conference on Learning Representations, 2022, https://openreview.net/forum?id=nZeVKeeFYf9., but still requires suitable training data, evaluation, and maintenance. It may also be unavailable for a particular foundation model. By contrast, engineers can often configure the harness and establish project-specific controls without modifying the model’s weights.

    MAGE concentrates on this second approach. The engineer acquires general-purpose intelligence as a service or software component, then engineers the environment in which that intelligence performs consequential work. The foundation model supplies general capabilities, the harness turns its outputs into actions and exposes points of control, and the governed engineering environment supplies the knowledge, obligations, and mechanisms governing those actions.

    REASONING

    Inset — Do LLMs Reason?

    Whether LLMs genuinely reason remains a subject of scientific debate. Bender and colleagues challenged the identification of linguistic fluency with understanding 66. Emily M. Bender et al., “On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots: Can Language Models Be Too Big?” Proceedings of the 2021 ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency, 2021, 610–23, https://doi.org/10.1145/3442188.3445922., while Bubeck and colleagues demonstrated broad problem-solving behavior in an early GPT-4 7.7. Sébastien Bubeck et al., Sparks of Artificial General Intelligence: Early Experiments with GPT-4, arXiv preprint arXiv:2303.12712, 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12712. More controlled experiments have found evidence of learned internal representations: studies of Othello-playing models recovered representations of board state and demonstrated their influence on predictions 898. Kenneth Li et al., “Emergent World Representations: Exploring a Sequence Model Trained on a Synthetic Task,” International Conference on Learning Representations, 2023, https://openreview.net/forum?id=DeG07_TcZvT.9. Neel Nanda et al., “Emergent Linear Representations in World Models of Self-Supervised Sequence Models,” Proceedings of the 6th BlackboxNLP Workshop: Analyzing and Interpreting Neural Networks for NLP (Singapore), 2023, 16–30, https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2023.blackboxnlp-1.2., while Jin and Rinard found evidence that models trained on programs had learned aspects of program semantics 10.10. Charles Jin and Martin Rinard, “Emergent Representations of Program Semantics in Language Models Trained on Programs,” Proceedings of the 41st International Conference on Machine Learning, Proceedings of Machine Learning Research, vol. 235 (2024): 22160–84, https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/jin24e.html. These studies establish different things: observable problem-solving behavior, learned structure, and evidence connecting internal representations to computation. None establishes that an agent will reason reliably about arbitrary production software. For this book, reasoning is a functional capability: using available information to draw inferences, evaluate alternatives, and work toward a solution. LLM-based systems can supply enough of that capability to undertake substantial engineering work. The question for engineering is how to put it to work under informed control.

    1.4.2 Four Properties of Commodity Intelligence

    Four properties of contemporary foundation models matter to the engineering argument.

    Broad semantic reasoning. A foundation model can interpret instructions, synthesize information, draw inferences, and search for solutions across open-ended domains. Give it an abstraction—a schema, state table, architectural model, or policy—and it can work with that abstraction rather than merely execute a fixed script. This makes it possible to delegate implementation decisions that engineers have not specified in advance. A plausible decision may nevertheless be inappropriate for the system at hand. An agent may select a familiar architecture or design pattern that works well elsewhere but violates a local constraint, introduces unnecessary complexity, or conflicts with an established design decision.

    Probabilistic output. Repeated invocations may produce different implementations or judgments. A foundation model’s confidence in its own result supplies no independent evidence of correctness. An agent may also fail consistently when its attempts share an incorrect assumption. Asking another foundation model to review the work can improve reliability, but the errors of the producer and evaluator may be correlated. Engineering assurance therefore cannot rest solely on the judgment that produced the result.

    Bounded working state. The information available to a foundation model during an invocation is finite. An agentic harness can extend its effective working state through retrieval, external memory, compaction, and repeated tool use, but information needed for a particular decision must be available when that decision is made. A long-horizon task requires carrying earlier decisions or commitments into later steps. Large software systems exceed what can be presented in a single reasoning episode, so agents must select and reconstruct relevant system knowledge. The reasoning horizon is the practical limit of the work a reasoner and its supporting machinery can carry through reliably, given the available representations and resources.

    Inexpensive repeated reasoning. Agents can explore alternatives at a speed and cost that human engineers cannot match. An agent can propose an implementation, run checks, revise its work, and try again, often many times in the time a human engineer would need for one attempt. This is particularly valuable when the engineer can state what constitutes an acceptable result. Human engineers are fallible reasoners too: they misunderstand requirements, overlook interactions, make incorrect assumptions, and introduce defects. Software engineering has long responded with specifications, types, tests, reviews, and other means of checking work independently of its author. Artificial reasoners introduce a new source of implementation capacity, not a new need for engineering assurance.

    These capabilities make natural language a remarkably productive interface for programming. In the practice known as vibe coding, a developer describes what an application should do, tries the generated software, and asks for changes in light of what happens. Some developers work almost entirely through that conversation. Others inspect or edit the code when a result surprises them 1112.11. Advait Sarkar and Ian Drosos, “Vibe Coding: Programming Through Conversation with Artificial Intelligence,” Proceedings of the 36th Annual Workshop of the Psychology of Programming Interest Group, 2025, https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.23253.12. Yi-Hung Chou et al., “Building Software by Rolling the Dice: A Qualitative Study of Vibe Coding,” Proceedings of the ACM on Software Engineering 3, no. FSE (2026), https://doi.org/10.1145/3797105. Either way, a developer can go from an idea to a working application with little code written by hand. For prototypes, this may be enough.

    On more complex systems, a change must also preserve requirements that nobody thought to repeat in the latest request. In experiments at Columbia University, applications that appeared to work mishandled business rules or lost track of state as users moved between components 13.13. Reya Vir, 9 Critical Failure Patterns of Coding Agents, DAPLab, Columbia University, 2026, https://daplab.cs.columbia.edu/general/2026/01/08/9-critical-failure-patterns-of-coding-agents.html. The FlowCheck study gives a particularly clear example. Three coding systems were asked to let several speakers share a time slot. All three also allowed a speaker to occupy several slots, violating an earlier rule 14.14. Reya Vir et al., “FlowCheck: Helping End-Users Specify and Verify Intent in Vibe-Coded Web Apps,” Proceedings of the 2nd ACM SIGPLAN International Workshop on Language Models and Programming Languages (Oakland, CA, USA), 2026, https://doi.org/10.1145/3843750.3843844. A developer who tried only the newly requested feature could easily miss what the change had undone.

    AGENT HARNESS

    Inset — Context Windows, Compaction, and the Reasoning Horizon

    A long-running engineering task can fail even when an agent has enough information for each step in isolation. An earlier decision or test result may be missing when the next step depends on it. In seqBench, reasoning horizon describes the length of a sequence a model can reliably complete 1515. Mohammad Ramezanali et al., “seqBench: A Tunable Benchmark to Quantify Sequential Reasoning Limits of LLMs,” Proceedings of the 2025 Conference on Empirical Methods in Natural Language Processing, 2025, 33771–92, https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2025.emnlp-main.1714.; here it includes what the harness and external resources make possible.

    A context window limits how much information a foundation model can process in one invocation. Instructions, conversation history, files, and tool results compete for space measured in tokens. A harness can carry work beyond one window by storing records for retrieval or using compaction: replacing accumulated context with a shorter account. Pages of investigation and test output might become a handoff recording the plan, decisions, checks, and remaining work. If the handoff says only “the tests passed,” the next agent cannot tell which tests ran or which version they exercised. Keeping the original results helps only if the agent retrieves them when needed 16.16. Prithvi Rajasekaran et al., “Effective Context Engineering for AI Agents,” Anthropic, September 29, 2025, https://www.anthropic.com/research/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents.

    The generation of commodity intelligence that motivated this book is built around language models with bounded context windows. The reasoning horizon is broader than a context-window limit and is intended to remain useful as new kinds of intelligence bring different capabilities and constraints.

    1.4.3 Steering Agents

    A foundation model supplies general reasoning capabilities, but the effectiveness of an agent also depends on how its harness organizes the work. A harness manages the context supplied to the foundation model, invokes tools in response to its outputs, and returns their results for further reasoning. It may also provide memory, task management, parallel execution, and hooks at which additional code can run. Engineers can use these facilities to influence both the information available to an agent and the sequence of actions through which it pursues a task.

    One approach, often called context engineering, concerns selecting and organizing the information available to an agent when it makes decisions 16. A foundation model’s context window is finite, so supplying irrelevant material consumes limited capacity and processing resources. Instructions, repository files, documentation, earlier decisions, and tool results must be selected and presented so that the agent can recover the knowledge it needs. Research on external representations has long shown that how information is organized affects the effort needed to reason with it 1718.17. Jill H. Larkin and Herbert A. Simon, “Why a Diagram Is (Sometimes) Worth Ten Thousand Words,” Cognitive Science 11, no. 1 (1987): 65–100, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1551-6708.1987.tb00863.x.18. Jiajie Zhang and Donald A. Norman, “Representations in Distributed Cognitive Tasks,” Cognitive Science 18, no. 1 (1994): 87–122, https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1801_3. A useful representation preserves relationships that would otherwise have to be reconstructed from implementation. Better representations can reduce that work without changing the underlying foundation model.

    Another approach is to structure agent behavior through feedback loops. Rather than asking an agent to produce a complete implementation in one attempt, the harness can organize repeated cycles of proposing changes, running tools, observing results, and revising the work 4. Tests, static analyses, and other checks can provide feedback that helps the agent improve successive attempts. The effectiveness of these loops depends on the evidence they produce: an agent may repeatedly pursue an incorrect assumption, optimize for an inadequate test, or satisfy a new request while losing an earlier requirement. Repeated attempts can improve a result, but they do not establish that the result is correct. Figure 1.4-2 illustrates these two approaches.

    1.4.4 A Simple Probabilistic Model of Success with Agents

    Commodity intelligence is probabilistic. This book focuses on the implications for engineering. A foundation model’s answer does not ordinarily come with a guarantee that its engineering judgments are correct. Generation can vary between runs, a request may admit several interpretations, and the model may choose different reasoning steps or tools. A task can depend on many such judgments before it produces a result. The reliability of the complete process also depends on how the harness and engineered environment organize the work.

    A simple model illustrates why reliability on individual features does not guarantee reliability across a product. Suppose an agent implements n features, with pi denoting the probability that feature i is implemented correctly. Under the simplifying assumption that these outcomes are independent, the probability that all features are correct is

    P(all features correct)=∏i=1npi

    When probabilities between zero and one are multiplied, their product decreases as more factors are added. If an agent implements each feature correctly with probability 99%, the probability that all three features are correct is 0.993≈97%. For ten features it is approximately 90%, and for one hundred it falls to approximately 37%. At 95% reliability per feature, the probability of implementing all ten correctly is approximately 60%. An agent that usually gets individual features right can therefore have a much lower probability of getting every feature in a product right.

    The independence assumption is deliberately simple; real implementation failures may be correlated, and a product’s features are not necessarily equally difficult. The calculation is not a prediction of agent performance. It illustrates why the reliability of the whole system cannot be inferred from success on individual tasks. Human review, automated checks, iterative repair, and more capable models can improve the final result, but their costs and limitations belong in the evaluation of the complete engineering process.

    The decisions that depend on agentic inference form the system’s probabilistic surface. Other parts may be deterministic, such as a tool that applies a fixed transformation or a test suite that evaluates explicit conditions. Better models and iterative loops can improve the reliability of inferred judgments. Engineers can also establish mechanisms that enforce selected requirements even when an agent makes a mistake. The engineering objective is not to eliminate probabilistic reasoning, but to decide where its uncertainty is acceptable and where stronger controls are needed.
