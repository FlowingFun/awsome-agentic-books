    §2.5 Decision Models: What Should Happen Next?

    A behavioral model can tell us that a failed job may be retried, escalated, or stopped. A decision model addresses the next question: given what we know about this failure, which alternative should we choose? A transient error may justify another attempt, while an exhausted retry budget may call for escalation. What makes this a decision model is that it represents the criteria for choosing among alternatives. The most useful form depends on the choice we face and the information available when we must make it.

    2.5.1 Conditions, Alternatives, and Decision Analysis

    Constructing a Decision Model

    Begin with one request. A service sells prepaid credits and also offers unlimited subscriptions. A customer requests work costing five credits. Should the service admit the request, and if so, should it charge five credits? To answer, we need to know which entitlement applies. For a prepaid customer we also need the balance. For an unlimited subscriber we need to know whether the work is covered.

    A decision table makes the cases easy to inspect when there are only a few. In Table 2.5-1, assume the request falls under exactly one entitlement. Read across the row whose conditions hold. A prepaid customer with five credits can proceed and spends the five; with four credits, the same request is rejected. For an unlimited subscription covering the work, the service admits the request without drawing on a balance.

    Table 2.5-1. Admission under one entitlement. Unlimited entitlement admits covered work without drawing down finite credit; a finite balance must cover the requested work.
    EntitlementRequested workDecision
    UnlimitedAny amount covered by the subscriptionAdmit without drawing down finite credit
    Finite balanceEnough credit for the requestAdmit and charge the required credit
    Finite balanceInsufficient credit for the requestReject for insufficient credit

    The input to the decision must preserve every distinction that can change its outcome. Suppose a caller supplies only the number of prepaid credits. A customer who has spent the last credit and an unlimited subscriber who never bought credits can both have a balance of zero. The table gives them different answers. No revision to a rule that sees only the balance can recover the missing entitlement.

    A decision tree arranges the same choice as a sequence of tests: first determine the entitlement, then consult the balance only on the prepaid branch. Rules can express exceptions and their precedence; constraints can describe which combinations of features may be selected together. For permissions, an access-control matrix records which operations a subject may perform on an object 1.1. Butler W. Lampson, “Protection,” ACM SIGOPS Operating Systems Review 8, no. 1 (1974): 18–24, https://doi.org/10.1145/775265.775268. When the alternatives differ in cost or performance, a quantitative model may help choose among them. Predicting a deployment’s latency supplies one input; selecting the least expensive deployment that meets a latency target uses that input to make a decision.

    A permission graph is useful when the decision concerns a relationship. In Figure 2.5-1, a web interface may call a report service, and the report service may call a renderer. To decide whether Web may call Renderer directly, look for that particular edge in the declared policy. The two-hop route through the report service grants no such permission. The observed graph records a direct Web-to-Renderer call. Comparing the two identifies a violation. If the observation had missed the call, its absence from the trace would tell us only what was recorded.

    Some decisions cannot be made from the object alone. Accessibility standards distinguish informative images, which need a text alternative, from decorative images, which assistive technology can ignore 2.2. World Wide Web Consortium, Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.1, 2018, https://www.w3.org/TR/2018/REC-WCAG21-20180605/. A blue square might decorate a restaurant menu but be the subject of a lecture on modern art.** For a real example, consider Yves Klein’s Blue Monochrome (1961), in the Museum of Modern Art’s collection 3.3. Museum of Modern Art, Yves Klein. Blue Monochrome. 1961, n.d., https://www.moma.org/collection/works/80103. What matters is the image’s contribution to the document, not merely what it depicts. A classifier can supply evidence about that role. The decision rule must say when the evidence warrants omitting a description and what to do when the role is uncertain.

    Properties and Analyses

    A decision table is complete if it prescribes an outcome for every case in its declared scope. To check Table 2.5-1, examine the balance below, exactly at, and above the requested amount. If the finite rows used only strict inequalities, a customer with exactly five credits requesting five credits would match neither row. Consistency asks whether applicable rules can prescribe incompatible outcomes. If both rows included equality, that customer would match both the admitting and rejecting rows unless a precedence rule settled the conflict. A request covered by several entitlements presents a different issue: the table assumes one. Extending the policy to such requests requires deciding how the entitlements combine.

    For a quantitative decision, first check admissibility: does an alternative satisfy every applicable constraint? Suppose a deployment must fit in 8 GB of memory and respond within 200 ms. A candidate requiring 9 GB is out, however fast or inexpensive it may be. Of the candidates that meet both limits, which costs least? That is an optimality question. A constraint solver can search for a feasible assignment; ranking feasible candidates by cost also requires an objective and comparable cost estimates. The least expensive candidate among those examined is an optimum over that set. A claim about all possible deployments needs a correspondingly wider analysis.

    Checking conformance asks whether a particular decision or observed action agrees with the declared policy. For the service graph, compare the observed edges with the permitted set. For the entitlement table, use the inputs to select a row and compare its verdict with the service’s actual response. Neither comparison settles whether the policy expresses the right judgment. A consistent policy could permit the very call the architect meant to exclude, just as an optimizer could minimize cost while omitting a requirement users care about. Engineers must examine the choice of inputs and criteria as well as the decisions made from them.

    2.5.2 DocAble: Policies and Decisions

    DocAble uses decision models for several purposes: deciding whether an image needs a description, declaring which services may communicate, and determining whether a user may upload a document. The representations differ because the decisions depend on different information. Each must preserve the distinctions that can change its answer.

    When is an image decorative?

    Accessibility remediation must distinguish images that convey information from those that are merely decorative. As the worked example illustrated, the same image can serve either purpose depending on its context. DocAble supplies a rendered slide to a vision-language model (VLM), which classifies each image or shape as clearly decorative, uncertain, or informational. The classification supplies evidence; the decision policy determines what to do with it. Only a clearly decorative verdict permits suppressing a description. An uncertain or missing verdict preserves the image’s informational role. This asymmetry is deliberate: an unnecessary description is audible, while wrongly hiding information can leave a screen-reader user unaware that anything was omitted.

    The need for an explicit uncertainty policy emerged from evaluation. Earlier classifiers had marked content-bearing images as decorative, particularly in visualization-heavy documents. We tightened the policy by representing uncertainty explicitly and making its conservative interpretation common to the classification paths. The decision model now states how uncertain evidence affects remediation without prescribing which VLM must supply that evidence. A different VLM can make the semantic judgment; it does not redefine the policy for acting on it.

    The remaining two DocAble examples concern permission. The system accepts documents for accessibility remediation, sends work among internal services, and returns corrected artifacts. Two decisions arise at different boundaries: which service may call another, and whether a user's entitlement permits a requested upload. Both can be modeled as permission, but they need different representations.

    Which services may communicate?

    DocAble distributes document remediation across several services. The public web tier receives requests, while internal services perform document editing, generation, rendering, OCR, and other work. Rather than specifying the permitted connections independently in deployment configuration, DocAble records them in a service-flow model. Each directed edge declares that one service may invoke another; an absent edge excludes that relationship from the declared policy.

    Service-flow model · Decision

    • Engineering question — Which service may call which, and what may be reached from outside?
    • Model — a declared graph of services and the permitted edges between them; the deployment is wired from the graph rather than the graph inferred from the deployment.
    • Property — internal communication is permitted only along a declared edge; one declared node is the public entry.
    • Quality attribute — least privilege; architectural integrity; security.

    Figure 2.5-2 shows a reduced view of the service-invocation policy. The web tier is the only publicly reachable service. Its declared outgoing edges permit calls to selected internal services. Separately, the worker may invoke generation, rendering, and OCR services. The arrows express permissions, not observed traffic or a requirement that every permitted call occur. Asynchronous job dispatch is omitted from this selected view; absence here does not imply prohibition by the full policy.

    The declared graph answers whether a proposed service invocation is permitted: look for the corresponding directed edge. It also supports reachability questions, such as which services the public web tier can reach through permitted calls. DocAble treats this graph as the source of truth for service-flow relationships and generates deployment configuration from its declared edges. A completeness check requires each gated edge to contain the information needed for deployment. These checks establish properties of the declaration and its generated configuration, not whether runtime traffic follows the intended policy.

    The declared graph can also serve as the policy against which observed service calls are checked. DocAble does not currently reconcile runtime traffic against that declaration. Whether to add such a check, and what consequences a violation should have, are Alignment decisions taken up in Chapter 3.

    Which entitlement authorizes an upload?

    The service-flow model determines which calls are permitted. Upload admission presents a different decision: whether a user’s entitlements authorize a particular document. This became a modeling problem when DocAble added unlimited-use groups to a system originally designed around finite credits.

    Table 2.5-1 assumed that exactly one entitlement applied to a request. DocAble’s domain is more complex: a user may belong to several groups, each with its own entitlement. The upload decision must account for the available finite credits and whether any applicable group grants unlimited use (Figure 2.5-3). The production failure arose when these facts traveled separately through the system.

    The original client upload rule was easy to understand from the code: if the user’s credits covered the upload cost, allow the upload; otherwise, reject it. Box (1) in Figure 2.5-4 shows this balance-based check. A separate decision model would have added little to understanding that simple rule.

    Unlimited groups changed what the upload decision had to account for. As box (2) shows, a group could authorize an upload without contributing finite credits to the balance. The server’s balance calculation, the client’s check before sending an upload, and the server admission gate represented entitlement in different ways. Understanding and updating the intended policy now required checking how each part of the implementation handled both finite and unlimited entitlement.

    Box (3) traces the production failure. In August 2026, a user whose personal group provided unlimited use was blocked from uploading a thirty-eight-page presentation: the upload needs 38 credits, and you have 0. The legacy server function read each group’s unlimited flag twice: once to exclude unlimited groups from the credit sum, and once to compute a display class. For this user it returned zero credits and the class individual. The login response omitted the detailed group records but carried a separate unlimited-status flag. The client upload check ignored that flag, compared the zero balance with the required 38 credits, and rejected the upload before sending the file. The server’s admission logic, which handled this entitlement correctly, was never reached. The failure came from using the finite-credit total as an admission decision even though other system representations retained the unlimited entitlement.

    The immediate fix made the client consult the unlimited-status flag it had been ignoring. That repaired the observed failure, but left the admission policy represented separately across the client and server. We therefore introduced the entitlement decision model shown in box (4): given the user’s group entitlements and the requested work, should the upload be admitted, and under which entitlement? Property tests checked the model, a cross-runtime parity corpus checked shared policy cases in the server and client, and another check validated the model’s shape. The display code derived its values from the model, while server admission delegated the policy decision to it.

    Entitlement decision model · Decision

    • Engineering question — May this user upload this document, and under which entitlement?
    • Model — per-group entitlements that keep finite and unlimited as distinct cases; the output is a structured admission decision: admitted or not, the group that authorizes it, and the ordered plan by which the verdict was reached.
    • Property — an uncapped unlimited group can authorize an upload with zero finite credits; the display projection does not decide admission.
    • Quality attribute — decision correctness; fidelity between the domain and its representations.

    The repaired model preserves the distinction that the earlier admission check lost. For the same uncapped unlimited group and 38-page request, it admits the upload and records the authorizing group and an admit_unlimited plan step, even though the finite credit balance remains zero. The display projection answers a different question: it returns a special numeric value for unlimited use, or a finite credit sum when all groups are finite. That display value cannot determine admission. The model can therefore be tested against cases involving finite credits, unlimited entitlement, and their combinations without treating a display value as the decision itself.

    A note in the first person: I changed the domain in one place — groups could now be unlimited — and did not carry the change into every representation of the same decision. An explicit model would not have made the change automatically correct; it would have made the inconsistency visible as an engineering obligation. When the domain contains a consequential distinction, every representation used to reason about that decision must be capable of expressing it. Chapter 3 gives the recurring pattern here — a failure converted into explicit structure that later work inherits — a name.

    The three decision models preserve different kinds of information: evidence about an image’s role, permitted relationships between services, and the entitlements that authorize an upload. Each makes a decision inspectable by separating the information used to make it from the criteria governing the outcome. That separation also makes it possible to revise a classifier, service topology, or entitlement scheme without silently redefining the decision policy.

    Decision models make the criteria for a choice explicit, whether the choice concerns permission or what to do next. The final section steps back from one representation to ask how a family of models can answer questions together.
